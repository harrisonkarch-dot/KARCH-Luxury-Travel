Seasonal Inspiration

Why the South of France feels so irresistible when it is done with the right rhythm.

The South of France is the destination people most often plan badly. Not because it is difficult — because it is easy to book and hard to pace. Five towns in seven days, a car you did not need, and a fortnight's worth of driving compressed into a week that was supposed to be restful.

Done properly it is the most effortless week in Europe. The difference is almost entirely about how few places you go.

Pick two bases. Not four.

This is the whole guide, really.

The coast between Saint-Tropez and Menton is under two hours end to end, which fools people into thinking they can move constantly. They can. It just ruins the trip — every hotel change costs you a morning and an evening, and four changes in eight nights means half the holiday spent in transit or unpacking.

Two bases, four nights each, is the shape I would argue for. Everything below is reachable from one of them as a day trip.

Cap d'Antibes or Cap-Ferrat — for the classic version

Quiet, green, and residential in the way the Riviera was before it was famous. Both caps put you within thirty minutes of Nice, Antibes, Villefranche and Monaco without any of them being outside your door. This is where I would start.

Saint-Tropez — for the livelier half

Better than its reputation if you go in the right weeks and stay slightly outside the town itself. Ramatuelle and the Pampelonne side give you beach club access without the port crowds, and the drive in for dinner is fifteen minutes.

Or go inland — Provence rather than the coast

Ménerbes, Gordes, Saint-Rémy. Cooler, quieter, less expensive, and in September genuinely more interesting than the coast because harvest is happening. If the point of the trip is food and landscape rather than sea and scene, this is the better half of the region and almost nobody splits their week this way.

How the days should actually run

Build around two meals. Everything else is optional.

The rhythm that works here is unusually specific: a slow breakfast, one thing in the middle of the day, a long lunch that becomes the afternoon, several hours doing nothing, then dinner late. That is the whole day. Attempting a museum, a market, a beach club and a village in the same twelve hours is how people come home tired.

Lunch matters more than dinner here, which surprises people. The best tables — beach clubs especially — are lunch institutions, and a two-hour lunch by the water is the single most characteristic thing you can do in the region.

The boat day

Worth it, if you do it once and do it properly.

A private day charter is the thing people remember, and the thing most under-booked. Not a group excursion — a small boat with a skipper for the day, out to the Îles de Lérins or along the coast, with lunch either aboard or at a beach you cannot reach by road.

One of these, mid-trip, resets the entire holiday. Two is unnecessary. Book it several weeks ahead in season, and keep the day flexible for weather.

When to go

September, then June. August only if you must.

September is the best month by a clear margin. The sea has had all summer to warm, the crowds go home after the first week, restaurants that would not take you in August suddenly will, and the light is the reason painters moved here.

June is the second choice — everything is open, nothing is full yet, and the evenings are long.

August is when the French holiday, the coast is at capacity, and prices peak. It is still beautiful. It is simply the hardest version of the same trip.
